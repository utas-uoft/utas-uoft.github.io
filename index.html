01

The gap

No course at U of T teaches agent evaluation.

AI agents went from demo to deployed in about eighteen months. The skills that make them work — designing tools, managing context, evaluating outputs, handling failure, controlling cost and latency — are what employers are hiring for right now.

There is no course on agent evaluation. There is no course on context engineering. Campus has not caught up, and there is no student group filling the gap.

So we're building one.

02

The distinction

Systems, not models.

Most AI work is

model-centric

Train a model. Read an architecture paper. Push a benchmark. It is research, and it requires a machine learning background to participate at all.

Agentic work is

systems-centric

Orchestration. Tool interfaces. Context. Evaluation. Failure modes. Cost and latency. It looks far more like reliability engineering than like machine learning.

Which is why the floor is low and the ceiling is high — and why we want law, commerce, life sci and humanities students in the room, not as an afterthought but because the work is worse without them.

03

What we do

Two halves, wired together.

Research

Agent Lab

Pods of three to five people. Six-week cycles. One public artifact per pod, per cycle — a writeup and a repo, published under the society's name.

Year one runs replication, then teardown, then eval-building. After two completed cycles, one pod graduates to original work aimed at a real venue.

Publish, or the pod dissolves. Cadence beats ambition.

Teaching

Agents in Practice

An open lecture series, free and drop-in, organised by sector — agents in law, in drug discovery, in finance operations, in public-service delivery.

Plus the Agent Engineering Track: six weeks, hands-on, capacity-limited, ending in a demo night.

No machine learning background required. Genuinely.

Why both

  1. Research pods ship artifacts
  2. Artifacts become teaching material
  3. The cohort's demo night recruits the next pods

Most two-track societies fail the same way: teaching absorbs all the labour and research quietly dies by November. Here each half is load-bearing for the other.

Founding team

We need seven people.

The University will not review a new society without a primary contact, two administrative officers, and five general members. That paperwork is the actual bottleneck between this existing and not existing.

Three to five hours a week, starting January. VP Events is the highest-leverage role in year one and requires no technical background at all.
